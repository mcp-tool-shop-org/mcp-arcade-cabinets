## The brief (the Director, 2026-09-15)

> The user is a lovable vibe coder whose requests are absurd ("make it more blockchain", "add AI to the button", "like Uber but for ducks"), the agent is a gleeful overachiever, and the payoff for typing the code is spectacle. You finish the snippet and the thing builds on screen, the deploy fires, the confetti and the meme reaction land, the score rolls up with a multiplier. Difficulty climbs as the requests get more unhinged and the code gets longer. Nothing yells at you. Failure is a funny "hmm, that's not it" and a retry, not a loss.
>
> Tone: gleeful and absurd (the human is comically absurd and the AI agent is hard-working, sycophantic and lovable). A real arcade scoreboard with combos and multipliers, whose points correlate with what's being built. Payoff is that something is built. Levels with an endless option. There should be a context timer, as that's a needed component and it's clever. Just needs to be more joyful than painful. With a painful hardcore mode for the skilled.

Two earlier drafts were refused for the record: one carried Ghost's grammar with a keyboard (refused as "the same game but typing"), one built its tension from a nagging user, shipped bugs and compaction (refused as "stressful, not fun"). The lock below is built from the brief above, not from either draft.

## Research grounding

Five questions, one Opus research agent each, run before the brief was final. Sources are named; where none was found the gap is stated. Findings are cited by question and number (Q1.3 is the third finding of question one). The transcription question (Q4) was asked for a lie mechanic this cabinet no longer has; its findings that still bear are kept, the rest are recorded as consulted and not load-bearing.

**Q1. What feedback and practice design measurably improves typing.** Winstein and Schmidt, "Reduced Frequency of Knowledge of Results Enhances Motor Skill Learning", JEP:LMM 1990 (https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.16.4.677): feedback on about half the trials, or faded, retained as well or better than feedback on every trial. Salmoni, Schmidt and Walter, "Knowledge of Results and Motor Learning", Psychological Bulletin 1984 (PubMed 6399752): the guidance hypothesis, frequent extrinsic feedback props up performance and blocks the learner's own error model. Logan and Crump, "Cognitive Illusions of Authorship Reveal Hierarchical Error Detection in Skilled Typists", Science 2010 (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1190483): typists slow after their own errors even when the screen hides them. Pinet and Nozari, "The Role of Visual Feedback in Detecting and Correcting Typing Errors", J. Memory and Language 2021 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2020.104193): hiding the typed text barely hurt detection and crushed correction. Dhakal, Feit, Kristensson and Oulasvirta, "Observations on Typing from 136 Million Keystrokes", CHI 2018 (https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174220): speed is predicted by cross-hand and cross-finger pair intervals and rollover, not single keys. Feit, Weir and Oulasvirta, "How We Type", CHI 2016 (https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858233): consistent finger-to-key mapping predicts speed; finger count does not. Etkin, "The Hidden Cost of Personal Quantification", J. Consumer Research 2016 (https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucv095): measuring an enjoyable activity raised volume and cut enjoyment. Baddeley and Longman, "The Influence of Length and Frequency of Training Session on the Rate of Learning to Type", Ergonomics 1978 (https://doi.org/10.1080/00140137808931764): one short session a day beat massed practice per hour. Wickens, Hutchins, Carolan and Cumming, "Effectiveness of Part-Task Training and Increasing-Difficulty Training Strategies", Human Factors 2013 (https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812451994): part-task drills transfer only for separable components. **Not found:** any evaluation of adaptive weak-key drilling in tutors; any controlled study of forced correction against free flow. _Implications:_ the scoreboard is the game's number, never the typist's metric: valuation, hype and streak sit on the field, words per minute and accuracy never do (Q1.2, Q1.7); the typed text is always rendered (Q1.4); a mistyped character needs no flash beyond its sound (Q1.3); the retro is opt-in at level end, not offered every level, and ranks consistency above speed (Q1.1, Q1.5, Q1.6); weak bigrams are seeded into the next level's real requests and never drilled bare (Q1.9); levels are short and a day's play is a few of them (Q1.8). See G23, G27.

**Q2. What makes a code snippet hard to type.** Dhakal et al. 2018 (above): cost lives in adjacent pairs; same-finger and same-hand pairs such as `))`, `::` and `""` are the expensive units. Feit et al. 2016 (above): symbols outside a player's stable mapping cost most. Logan and Crump, "Hierarchical Control of Cognitive Processes: The Case for Skilled Typewriting", Psychology of Learning and Motivation 2011 (https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-385527-5.00001-2): an outer loop emits words and an inner loop types chunks it knows; `function` is a chunk, `"},{"` is not. Behmer and Crump, "Crunching big data with finger tips", 2017 (https://www.crumplab.com/EntropyTyping/articles/EntropyTyping_Draft.html): inter-key interval tracks the entropy of the next character given context. Buse and Weimer, "Learning a Metric for Code Readability", IEEE TSE 2010 (https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2009.70): identifier count, line length and punctuation density are the strongest predictors; Scalabrino et al. 2018 (https://doi.org/10.1002/smr.1958) find nesting depth weak. Karrenbauer and Oulasvirta, "Improvements to Keyboard Optimization with Integer Programming", UIST 2014 (https://doi.org/10.1145/2642918.2647382): layout cost is the sum over bigrams of frequency times key-pair travel. Hellendoorn, Proksch, Gall and Bacchelli, "When Code Completion Fails", ICSE 2019 (https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE.2019.00101) with Marasoiu et al., PPIG 2015: developers lean on completion most for long identifiers, so those are the least practised. Advisory: carpalx (https://mkweb.bcgsc.ca/carpalx/) three-term effort model; keybr (https://www.keybr.com/help) introduces one character at a time behind per-key thresholds. **Not found:** a keystroke dataset filtered to code; measured bracket-pair cost in code. _Implications:_ a request's value is a deterministic formula over its code: summed per-character surprisal from a character n-gram model built over the corpus (Q2.4), plus bigram travel cost from a fixed key map (Q2.1, Q2.6), plus line length and punctuation density (Q2.5), with long identifiers and closing brackets weighted up (Q2.7) and nesting depth ignored. The value is the score the request pays and the axis the levels climb on. See G24, G26.

**Q3. Arcade feel and audio as the primary channel.** Kao, "The effects of juiciness in an action RPG", Entertainment Computing 2020 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1875952118300879): with 3,018 players, no juice and extreme juice both cut play time, experience and performance against medium and high. Hicks, Gerling, Richardson, Pike and Dickinson, "Juicy Game Design", CHI PLAY 2019 (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3311350.3347171): embellishments raise appeal and sense of agency. Smets and van der Spek, "That Sound's Juicy!", ICEC 2021 (https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-89394-1_24): juicy audio alone raised presence. Sigrist, Rauter, Riener and Wolf, "Augmented visual, auditory, haptic, and multimodal feedback in motor learning: a review", Psychonomic Bulletin and Review 2013 (https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0333-8): pitch is the validated mapping for continuous performance feedback and its gains persist when withdrawn. Albert, Burkard, Queck and Herrlich, "The Effect of Auditory-Motor Synchronization in Exergames", PACM HCI 2022 (https://doi.org/10.1145/3549516): synchrony helps only when the player perceives it. Clark, Lawrence, Astley-Jones and Gray, "Gambling Near-Misses Enhance Motivation to Gamble", Neuron 2009 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2008.12.031): near-misses raise the urge to continue while being less pleasant. Cassidy and MacDonald, "The effects of music on time perception and performance of a driving game", Scand. J. Psychology 2010 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20602740/): fast tempo raises speed and errors together. Zohaib, "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in Computer Games: A Review", Advances in HCI 2018 (https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/5681652): adjust on observable inputs; players read a mid-round change of the task itself as cheating. Advisory: Swink, Game Feel, 2008; Jonasson and Purho, "Juice it or lose it", 2012; Hicks et al., "Good Game Feel", DiGRA 2018; Collins, Playing with Sound, 2013. **Not found:** near-miss or streak effects in a skill game; visible against hidden ramps; anything peer-reviewed on ZType, The Typing of the Dead or Epistory. _Implications:_ the feel budget is high, not extreme: shake capped and scaled to line length, one stinger per event class not per keystroke, the full flash reserved for the ship (Q3.1); every correct character throws a located tracer (Q3.2); every event has a layered sound with a transient and a tail (Q3.3); the streak is a semitone climb on the keystroke sample, reset on a miss, capped at an octave and held, beside the visible multiplier (Q3.4); the bed's tempo rises with hype and holds through the last request of a level (Q3.7); the only mid-level ramp is the context drain rate (Q3.8); a request shipped with the context bar nearly empty is authored as a near-miss with its own low sound (Q3.6). See G25, G29.

**Q4. Whether copy-typists read what they type (consulted; the mechanic it served is gone).** Salthouse, "Perceptual, Cognitive, and Motoric Aspects of Transcription Typing", Psychological Bulletin 1986 (https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.99.3.303): typists transmit characters on a seven-to-eight character span and comprehension is unrelated to skill. Sanford, Sanford, Molle and Emmott, "Shallow Processing and Attention Capture in Written and Spoken Discourse", Discourse Processes 2006 (https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326950dp4202_2): italics in text and stress in speech raise anomaly detection. Wolfe, Horowitz and Kenner, "Rare items often missed in visual searches", Nature 2005 (https://doi.org/10.1038/435439a): misses climb as targets get rare. _Implications kept:_ scope creep and the user's asides are shown and voiced before they are typeable, never appended silently inside a line the player is already transcribing (Q4.1, Q4.2). Nothing else from Q4 carries a lock line.

**Q5. Comedic framing and the workday.** Dormann and Biddle, "A Review of Humor for Computer Games", Simulation and Gaming 2009 (https://doi.org/10.1177/1046878109341390): humor works by smoothing mechanics, softening failure and carrying exposition. Gagnon et al., "Exploring players' experience of humor and snark in a grade 3–6 history practices game", GLS 2022, an RCT of 11,804 players (https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.09906): script variants moved perceived humor and character likeability, not completion. Habgood and Ainsworth, "Motivating Children to Learn Effectively", J. Learning Sciences 2011 (https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2010.508029): fiction fused with the mechanic beats a sugar coat. Cordova and Lepper, "Intrinsic Motivation and the Process of Learning", J. Educational Psychology 1996 (https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.4.715): contextualisation with light personalisation raised engagement and learning. McGraw and Warren, "Benign Violations", Psychological Science 2010 (https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610376073): a violation amuses only while it is benign. Ferguson and Ford, "Disparagement humor", HUMOR 2008 (https://doi.org/10.1515/HUMOR.2008.014): in-group targeting lands when it signals shared membership. Tsakona, "Intertextuality and/in political jokes", Lingua 2018 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lingua.2017.09.003): an unrecognised allusion excludes. Yan et al., "Assessing the Capabilities of LLMs in Humor", 2025 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09133) and Bhatt et al., "Can AI Take a Joke, Or Make One?", C&C 2025 (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3698061.3734388): model humor sits below mid-tier humans and skews to novelty. Advisory: Beaulieu, "Functions and powers of barks in video games", DiGRA 2020: shallow bark pools turn hostile; swap whole sets by context. **Not found:** humor and retention in two-minute sessions; measured meme shelf life. _Implications:_ the words typed are the work (Q5.3); the player names their agent and the user addresses it (Q5.4); the user is demanding and absurd but never cruel (Q5.5); the jokes punch at the situation both share (Q5.6); the durable pool carries the lines and dated memes sit in a small versioned set the seed skips by default (Q5.7); every punchline, request and reaction is authored; a seated model writes only high-churn filler through the gate (Q5.8); line sets are keyed to level tier and beat, never repeat inside a level, and retire once seen twice (Q5.9); the typing loop carries retention and the humor makes the user someone you want to keep answering (Q5.2). See G28.

## The lock, G23 to G30

Inherited without change: G8 (no ranking against other players; the only best is the browser's own), G12 and G14 where a model sits (fact-blind in, gated out), G17 (a seated model is a character, nothing on the field names it), G18 (packaging) when the cabinet reaches the container.

**G23. The scoreboard is the game's number, never the typist's.** Valuation, hype (the multiplier) and streak are on the field at all times and roll up with sound. Words per minute, accuracy and error counts never appear on the field. The retro at level end is opt-in, is not offered after every level, shows weak keys before speeds, ranks consistency above raw speed, and compares only against this browser's own past. (Q1.1, Q1.2, Q1.5, Q1.6, Q1.7; G8.)

**G24. Points correlate with what is built.** Every request carries a value computed by the deterministic difficulty formula over its code (surprisal, bigram travel, length, punctuation density, identifier and closing-bracket weight). Shipping the request pays that value times the current hype, and adds a piece to the build whose size is the same value. The preview grows by exactly what the score counts. Milestones on valuation (seed, series A, unicorn) are stingers, not rules. (Q2.1 to Q2.7.)

**G25. Nothing yells.** A mistyped character sounds and colours the character, drops the streak, and waits for a backspace. A line sent with an error returns the agent's own "hmm, that's not it", resets that line, and costs nothing else. The context bar reaching empty in a level is a compaction: a one-line summary card in the agent's voice, hype reset, bar refilled, play continues. In endless it ends the run with the valuation on the board. No lives, no walk-off, no counter of failures anywhere. Only hardcore hurts, and only from the selector. (Q1.3, Q3.1, Q5.1, Q5.5.)

**G26. The context timer is the pace.** The bar drains at the level's rate; each user message costs a fixed slice; shipping a request compacts and refills a share set by the level. Mid-level, the drain rate is the only thing that ramps. The request order, each request's code and each request's value are fixed at level start from the seed. Hardcore: a faster drain, every mistyped character burns context, no Copilot, and empty ends the level. (Q3.8; the Director's word on the timer.)

**G27. Difficulty is a formula and levels climb on it.** `levels.json` names each level's stack (a language), its request count, its band range and its drain; the seed picks requests from the corpus inside the band, biased toward this browser's weak bigrams and never toward a repeat. Endless chains levels with rising bands and drain until the bar empties. (Q1.9, Q2.5, Q3.8.)

**G28. The user and the agent are authored data behind the gate; in endless, the user may be a model.** `user.json` holds the requests, the reactions and the asides by tier and beat; `agent.json` holds the agent's replies, its "hmm", its compaction summaries and its ship lines. Every line passes the same load-time scan as `voice.json` (no digit, no forbidden word) and no line repeats inside a level. Dated memes carry `dated: true` and are skipped unless the player turns them on. Levels are authored end to end. In endless mode a seated model plays the user as a lever fill: the sim offers a closed choice of corpus snippets in band, the model returns one pick and one ask line through the say gate, and may write reactions and asides; it never writes the code the player types, never a punchline in a level, and a late or refused answer is the authored pool. The next ask is prefetched during the current request; the sim never waits (G11, G13). The seat is reached two ways: the launcher's tiered node-side seat (Claude by API key, else an Ollama cloud tag, else local) and the container's stdio tools `product`, `ask`, `react`, so any MCP client can sit in the user's chair. Pages omits the seat. (Q5.1, Q5.5 to Q5.9; G11 to G14, G17; the Director's word of 2026-09-15 on the endless adapter.)

**G29. The feel budget is high, not extreme, and the sound is the score's voice.** One layered sample per event class; keystroke pitch climbs a semitone per clean line to an octave and holds; the tracer, the build-piece pop, the ship flash and the confetti each have one sound; shake is capped and scaled; the bed's tempo follows hype and holds through a level's last request; spawns and refills land on the bar with a legible hit. (Q3.1 to Q3.5, Q3.7.)

**G30. Tapes season; they never schedule.** The integration stack draws real server and tool names from the tapes on disk so a request can name a thing the player runs. The cabinet reads tape headers and rows through `tape-core` and nothing else, never a receipt, never a fact into play, and never a server at play time. (G1.)

## The game

**The field.** A chat pane where the user speaks in blue and the agent replies; an editor where the request's code sits as ghost text and fills in as the player types; a preview pane where the product assembles piece by piece; a scoreboard with valuation, hype and streak; the context bar. DOM for the chat and the editor (text rendering, IME, screen readers); canvas for the preview, the tracers and the confetti. The headless sim knows nothing of either.

**A level.** A product the user wants ("Uber but for ducks"). The user sends requests in turn. Each request is one beat: the agent's reply line (short prose, typed; it is the sycophancy, and it is also a warm-up of easy bigrams), then the code (the snippet, typed line by line; Enter sends a line). Shipping the request compacts the context, pays the value, pops a piece into the preview, and the user reacts. The last request of a level is the deploy: a `git push` line, the ship flash, the confetti, the user's review, the level's valuation. Then the next level's product.

**Endless.** The same loop with rising bands and drain, product names drawn from `products.json` (a noun list and a template list), until the bar empties.

**Hardcore.** From the selector only. G26's numbers. The agent's lines stay kind; the bar does not.

**Arcade components (slice 1 unless marked).** Streak and hype (the multiplier climbs by streak steps set in `score.json`, drops to one on a miss). Copilot: at a streak threshold the editor offers a ghost completion for a bounded time; Tab takes the rest of the line for reduced value. Scope creep: the user's "oh also can it…" appends a short line to the request, shown and voiced before it is typeable. Quick sync (slice 2): a between-requests interrupt of short chat lines, mechanically a breather. Near-miss: a ship with the bar nearly empty gets its own low sound. Milestones: valuation stingers at thresholds from `score.json`.

**What the player learns.** The corpus is dev-op-typer's calibration set (six languages, seven bands, five per band, with teaching notes) plus its topic snippets, ported to `patterns/corpus/<lang>.json`. The retro lists what shipped with the notes beside each piece. Weak bigrams from this browser's history bias the next level's picks. Ten stacks at most: one per corpus language, plus the integration stack from tapes.

**The seat (slices 3 and 4).** In endless, a model plays the user: it picks the next snippet from a closed in-band choice and phrases the ask, through the say gate, with the authored pool as fallback and the next ask prefetched. It never writes code. Pull path in slice 3 (the launcher's tiered seat), push path in slice 4 (the container's `product`, `ask`, `react` tools for any MCP client). The voice worker speaks the user's lines. Nothing on the field names the model.
